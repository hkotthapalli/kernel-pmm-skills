# Case studies

The most requested and least produced asset in product marketing. The market prices the writing:
$750 at Uplift Content, a $3,800 written bundle at Testimonial Hero, $800 to $2,000 typical across
the 2026 market. But the writing is the easiest 20% of the job. The bottleneck nobody sells a
service for is the other 80%: finding a customer whose result is real, getting a number that
survives a challenge, and securing permission in writing. This output is mostly about those three.

One move the specialist market got right is kept: **four formats from one interview**. Full story,
one-pager, deck slide, quote card. The interview is the expensive part; everything made from it is
nearly free.

## Two deliverables

1. **The story kit** — what the client runs after handover:
   - The candidate pipeline sheet, 5 to 8 names, never one
   - The interview guide the founder can run a call from
   - The number-workaround ladder
   - The permission record template
2. **The report** — the pipeline judged, the formats planned, the finding.

For demo use, the report carries a miniature of the kit.

## The pipeline sheet

Score every candidate on four criteria, in order:

1. **Quantified** — is there a measurable result? Without one the asset is a testimonial, which is
   weaker and must be labelled as what it is.
2. **Representative** — either a name that carries weight or a company that looks exactly like the
   segment being sold to. The lookalike usually persuades more than the logo.
3. **Willing** — the relationship and internal appetite exist.
4. **Recent** — within 18 months. Older results describe a product that has moved.

Five to eight candidates, because attrition through legal, timing and job changes is high, and a
one-candidate process stalls a quarter when that champion changes role. Source them cheapest
first: usage data, delighted reviewers (pre-qualified and almost never mined), renewal
conversations, closed-won notes.

**Ask the champion, not procurement**, and make the ask small: one 30-minute call, we write
everything, you approve every word, anonymous version as the fallback. The line that persuades
most champions: the finished story is also the document their own team uses internally to justify
the purchase.

## The interview guide

30 to 45 minutes, recorded with permission. Before (what was it costing) · the trigger (why act
when you did — the most skipped and most valuable question) · evaluation (what else was considered
— feeds the win/loss log and battlecards) · what went badly (one honest friction point makes the
whole story believable) · the result, in numbers, and **how they measured it** · what they would
tell someone considering this. The best quotes come from the pause after the prepared answer.

## The number ladder

When the customer will not share figures, work down, strongest first:

relative change without the base → time saved per person per week → a ratio ("three times the
plans reviewed by the same person") → avoided cost → speed ("three days to two hours") → **their
own internal metric** (already sanctioned, shared most readily) → specific-qualitative ("the 6am
calls stopped").

Always record the measurement method. A number without a method falls to the first prospect who
challenges it, and takes the whole story down with it. **If there is genuinely no number, ship a
testimonial and label it one.** Never manufacture a metric.

The root cause of most missing numbers sits upstream: nobody wrote down the before at signing.
The fix is the onboarding journey's baseline record, and the report says so when it applies.

## The permission record

The step that kills more case studies than any other. Per story: approver name and role · date ·
scope (web, sales, paid, events) · logo permission separately · expiry or renewal condition ·
**customer still a customer, checked quarterly**. A story from a churned customer is worse than no
story; a prospect will find out. Written approval only — verbal approval does not survive a
personnel change.

## The four formats

From each interview: the full story (one page, result first, their words, one honest friction
point) · the one-paragraph version for decks and proposals · the deck slide (three to five proof
fragments) · the quote card. Structure of the full story: who → the before and the trigger → what
they tried → why this → what happened → the number with its method → the quote → now.

## Guardrails

- **Never fabricate or composite quotes. Never round a number upward.**
- "After X, Y improved" is honest; "X caused Y" usually is not, at small scale.
- Anonymous-and-specific beats named-and-vague, and is a legitimate outcome, not a failure.
- Steady pipeline beats a pre-launch burst; stories requested under launch pressure are the ones
  whose permission does not arrive in time.
- Proof is stage-matched: logos work early, quantified stories at evaluation, live references and
  certifications at the decision. A story shown at the wrong stage persuades nobody.

## The report

Header: `Case studies · N candidates · N with a number · <date>`.

**01 · The pipeline, scored** — candidates against the four criteria in fill grammar; the gaps are
the finding, not a failure to hide.

**02 · The number problem** — where each candidate's number would come from on the ladder, and
what blocks it.

**03 · The finding** — one sentence, black band.

**04 · Four from one** — the format plan for the strongest candidate, drawn as four miniature
artefacts.

**05 · What can't be known** — always including: willingness is asserted until asked; a number's
method is unknown until the interview; and permission is nothing until it is written.

## Drawing rules

- Pipeline as a scored grid, fill grammar: solid = holds, half = partial, dashed = unknown,
  struck = disqualified.
- The four formats drawn as miniatures the reader recognises as documents.
- Fragments in drawings. Counts, never percentages under thirty.

## Language

| Use | Never |
|---|---|
| Story, case study | Success story asset, advocacy content |
| The number and how it was measured | Impressive metrics |
| A testimonial, labelled | A case study without a number |
| Who approved, when, for what | Permission secured |

## Standing rules for every output

- **Two deliverables: the kit the client runs, and the report.**
- **A story without a number is a testimonial and says so.**
- **Nothing is published without a written approval record.**
- **No invented findings. No false zeroes.**
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
- **Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes. No balanced two-part lines.** Written for a
  cold reader.
