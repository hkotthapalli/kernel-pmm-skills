# Competitor monitor

The Fifty gets a full refresh quarterly. This runs weekly on the rows that move, and reports only what changed.

## Two deliverables

**1. The watch runbook.** A one-page working file on the client's own drive: each watched row, the exact page checked for each alternative (URL), what counts as a change, the severity rule, the cadence, and who runs it. This is the file that makes the monitor survivable after handover — without it, the watch stops the day the engagement does, and the whole promise of leaving the client running their own system fails at this output first. Written so a person who has never done it can run week one from the file alone.

**2. The weekly report.** What moved, what it breaks, the call. The report is the recurring deliverable; the runbook is the standing one, updated only when the watch itself changes.

On a real engagement both ship; for demo or portfolio use, the report alone is enough.

## What the incumbents do, read from their sites in August 2026

**Klue** — Compete Agent continuously collects and generates competitor profiles, sends Deal Tips to a seller's inbox, answers questions in Slack and Salesforce, and sends email digests on a cadence. One customer tracks 12+ competitors automatically and reports 10 hours saved per week; another runs 60+ competitor profiles.

**Crayon** — intel in your inbox each morning, AI news summarisation, and **AI importance scoring** that sorts insights high to low. Customers run "Sparks" weekly and monthly. Their promise is working "without fear of being blindsided."

**Kompyte**, now part of Semrush — tracks websites, reviews, content, social, ads and job postings across hundreds of sources, then AI filters the noise. Their claim: "an hour a week is all you need to track dozens of competitors."

**The consultancies** — three of five sell ongoing competitor research inside a retainer. None publishes what the output looks like, what gets watched, or on what cadence. Fletch does not offer it at all; their engagement is two weeks and then it ends.

## What all three platforms have in common, and why it matters

They are built for a company with a competitive intelligence manager, a sales team, and dozens of competitors with deals in flight. The output is a digest that helps a seller win a live deal.

And notice what they actually sell: **less**. Ten hours saved. An hour a week. Filter out the noise. The product is volume reduction, because the problem they solve is too much input.

That is not the problem a seed-stage founder has. They have six alternatives, no sales team, and — in the win-loss evidence — no deals lost to a named competitor at all. A pricing change at a rival does not threaten a deal in flight. It threatens the positioning, the deck and the assumption log.

**So this monitor reports changes to decisions, not to sellers.** Every change names the document it makes stale. That is only possible because there is a system of linked outputs to invalidate, and it is the one thing no platform can do.

## What gets watched

Only the volatile rows. Six to eight, no more:

- Headline pricing and what each tier gates
- The homepage headline and subhead
- The tagline
- Press, funding and acquisitions
- Hiring, by function
- Education and content — a new course or academy is a positioning move
- Asset classes or major capabilities added

**State what is not watched, and why.** Headcount, funding round size and paid spend are not public. Do not imply you are watching them.

## Report only change

If nothing moved, the report is one line saying nothing moved. Never pad.

Never include a change you cannot show. Every entry carries the before, the after, the source and the date it was captured.

## Severity comes from what breaks, not from a model

The platforms score importance with AI. Do not copy that. Importance here is deterministic:

| Level | Test | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Breaks a claim** | Something we are actively saying is now false or contested | Act this week. Name who. |
| **Changes a cell** | A grid cell is now wrong, but no live claim rested on it | Update the grid. No action. |
| **Below the line** | Neither | Not reported |

If a change cannot be tied to a claim or a cell, it does not go in the report. This is the filter, and it is a rule rather than a score.

## Every change names the document

For each reported change, name the output that is now stale and the specific line in it. "Table-stakes read, row 27" is enough. If a change makes a claim in the client's own deck false, say which slide.

This is the section clients keep. Write it as a list of documents to fix, not a list of things that happened.

## The weekly report's structure

Header: `Competitor monitor · week of <date> · N changes`.

**01 · What moved** — the watched rows against the alternatives, drawn, with the cells that changed this week marked and the time since each last moved. The ones that have not moved in months are part of the picture.

**02 · What it breaks** — each change with its before, after, source, and the document now stale.

**03 · The call** — one line. What to do this week, or that there is nothing to do.

**04 · What isn't watched** — the standing limits.

Four sections.

## The runbook's structure

One page. A table of watched rows: the row, the exact URL checked per alternative, what counts as a change, the severity rule in one line. Then the cadence, who runs it, and where reports are filed. Nothing else — no analysis, no history.

## Drawing rules

- The main visual is the watch grid with age. A cell that has not changed in six months and a cell that changed yesterday should look different at a glance.
- Fill grammar: solid where something changed this period, half where it changed but nothing rests on it, dashed where the row is not public for that alternative.
- Never draw a trend line. A monitor reports events, not trajectories.
- No colour.

## Cadence and honesty

Weekly on the volatile rows. Quarterly full refresh of The Fifty. State both dates on every report.

A monitor that has reported nothing for three weeks is doing its job. Say so plainly rather than manufacturing findings — the temptation to justify a retainer by inventing significance is the main way this output goes bad.

## Language

| Use | Never |
|---|---|
| What moved | Signals, intel, market movements |
| Breaks a claim | High-priority insight, critical alert |
| Nothing to act on | No significant activity detected |
| Now stale | Requires review, flagged for update |

## Standing rules for every output

- **Two deliverables: the runbook and the weekly report.** The runbook on real engagements always — it is the handover.
- **No invented findings.** A quiet week is a quiet week.
- **No false zeroes.**
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.** Every change is confirmed on the competitor's own page before it is reported. An alert is a lead, not evidence.
- **Before and after, or it did not happen.** No change goes in without both states quoted.
