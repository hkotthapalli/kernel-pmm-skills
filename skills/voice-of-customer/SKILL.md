# Voice of customer

The calls the company already has, then six recorded customer interviews, turned into themes with counts, the language bank, the status quo that beats you, and the unmet expectations.

The method is the switch interview: you walk backwards through the decision to change, not forwards through opinions. People are unreliable about what they want and reliable about what they did.

## The deliverables

Three, not one.

**1. The interview guide.** A one-page working file: the recruiting ask, the rules, the six timeline moments with their questions, and the coverage checklist. Written so the founder or a team member can run interview seven themselves after the engagement ends. This is the handover. The research capability stays with the client, not with the consultant. It is also the answer to the fair objection that early-stage founders should not outsource talking to customers: the consultant runs the first six and hands over the method, the founder runs every one after that.

**2. The language bank.** A separate living file of the exact phrases, each with its count, its evidence source (a sales call, an interview or a public review) and the speaker's role, never a name. Separate because it outlives the report: the messaging work, the website and the ads all draw from it for months, and new phrases get added as new interviews happen. If it is buried in the report it dies with the report.

**3. The report.** Interview mix, themes, the status quo, unmet expectations, the key finding, the language bank, the limitations.

On a real engagement all three ship. For demo or portfolio use, the report alone is enough.

## Step zero: existing material

Before booking anyone, read the material that already exists. Most companies at seed to Series B have it and have never coded it.

- The last 20 sales-call recordings or transcripts, or all of them if fewer.
- Lost-deal notes, loss reasons in the CRM, and any churn conversations.
- Support tickets and inbound messages for the last 90 days.
- Exports from survey tools and helpdesks the client already uses.
- Public reviews of the company and its competitors, from sources Kernel can read in full (app stores, Hacker News and similar). Review sites that block reading are skipped, never summarised from a snippet.

Three uses, in order:

1. **Seed the language bank.** Pull exact phrases now, tagged with their evidence source. This is the fastest win in the whole output and it is often what a founder expected "customer research" to mean.
2. **Draft the hypotheses** (next section) with real material behind them rather than guesses.
3. **Choose who to recruit.** The recordings show recent customers and lost prospects. They cannot show the status quo.

That last limit is the reason the interviews exist. A sales recording only contains people who came to the company. The person who has the problem and has taken no action never books a call, and in most early markets that person is the largest group the company is losing to. Mining calls is the right first step and the wrong last one.

## Before the interviews

### Hypotheses, sealed

Before anyone is booked, two people each write down three hypotheses about what customers will say, separately, and seal them.

- **The founder's three.** This is the one that matters. Unmet expectations measure the gap between what the company believes and what the market believes, and the company's beliefs live in the founder.
- **Your three.** Sealed separately, so the report can say whose blind spot each one was.

After the interviews you open both lists and check which hypotheses never came up.

This is the only way to produce the unmet expectations section honestly. Do it first or it isn't a finding, it's hindsight.

### Interview mix

Six people minimum. The mix matters more than the number.

| How many | Segment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Recent customers, bought or started in the last 90 days | Memory is still accurate |
| 2 | Lost prospects, looked and didn't buy | They tell you what the customers won't |
| 1 | Status quo, in the market and has done nothing about the problem | The real competitor |

The status quo interview is the one nobody else runs. Doing nothing beats every competitor in most early markets, and the only way to understand it is to talk to somebody who is doing it. This interview is coded on its own (see "The status quo" below) because it answers a different question from the other five.

If the company has no customers yet, replace the recent customers with people who have the problem and have tried another solution.

### Recruiting

The founder makes the introduction, always. In our engagements a cold approach from a consultant mostly goes unanswered; a warm introduction from the founder mostly gets a yes.

The ask, sent by the founder:

> Can I borrow 30 minutes? We're trying to understand how people in your position handle [the problem]. Not a sales call, nothing to buy. [Name] is doing the interviews.

Offer no incentive to customers. Offer one to non-customers if you must.

### Recruiting and interviewing in the Gulf

The method holds. The mechanics change.

- **The ask goes by WhatsApp, from the founder's own number**, as a short text or a voice note. A calendar invite from a stranger is ignored; a voice note from someone they know is answered the same day.
- **Run the interview in the language the person thinks in.** If that is Arabic, use a named interpreter who is briefed on the rules, and transcribe in the original before translating. The language bank keeps the Arabic phrase and the translation side by side.
- **The competition domain must include relationships.** In this market the status quo usually looks like a WhatsApp group, a cousin's supplier, or a personal relationship that absorbs the problem. Ask for the current solution by name, not by category.
- **The status quo runs longer here.** A relationship hides the cost a tool would expose, so ask how long the workaround has been in place and what it has cost, in numbers, before asking what would change it.
- **Politeness is a source of false positives.** People will not tell someone senior that an idea is bad. The rules below already ban describing the product; in this market, enforce them harder.

### Set up

- 30 to 45 minutes. Recorded, with permission asked at the start.
- Transcribe. You need the exact words, not your notes.
- No slides, no demo, no product talk.

## The interview

### The rules

- **Ask about the past, never the future.** "What did you do?" not "would you use?"
- **Never describe the product.** The moment you pitch, the data is worthless.
- **Never ask if they like the idea.** Everybody says yes.
- **Chase the specific.** "Tell me about the last time that happened."
- **Sit in the silence.** The second answer is the true one.
- **Ask "why" no more than twice.** After that people invent reasons.

### The timeline

Walk it backwards from now to the first thought. Six moments.

**1. First thought**
When did you first realise the way you were doing this wasn't working?
What was happening around that time?

**2. The long nothing**
What did you do about it then?
(Usually nothing. Find out how long nothing lasted, why, and what it cost.)

**3. The trigger**
What changed that made you actually start looking?
Was there a specific day, meeting, or number?

**4. Looking**
What did you look at? In what order?
Who else was involved by then?
What almost made you stop?

**5. Choosing**
What made you pick this one?
What was the last thing you needed to know before you said yes?
Who had to approve it?

**6. After**
What happened in the first week?
What would make you go back to how you did it before?

For the status quo interview, moments 3 to 6 have not happened. Spend the time on 1 and 2, and add: what would have to be true for you to start looking, and what has stopped that from being true so far.

### Coverage across the six interviews

Not every interview covers everything. Across all six, you need all of these.

| Domain | |
|---|---|
| Pain | What is actually going wrong, in their words |
| Symptoms | How they'd notice it, if nobody told them |
| Value | What changes for them if it's fixed |
| Purchase trigger | What makes somebody start looking |
| Channels | Channels, people, content they trust |
| Economic buyer | Whose budget, and who else has to agree |
| Buying process | How a purchase actually happens here |
| Competition | Including the status quo, and including relationships |
| Cost of the status quo | What it costs, and why that cost has been bearable |

Track coverage as you go. If three domains are still empty after interview four, change the questions for interviews five and six.

## After the interviews

### Transcription and coding

- Transcripts come from the recording tool, in the original language.
- The first pass of grouping quotes into candidate themes can be done by a model working from the transcripts. It is fast and it is a draft.
- Every candidate theme is then checked by a person against the transcript before it gets a count. A theme that cannot be traced to exact lines in at least two transcripts does not ship. The model drafts, the person approves.

### Coding

1. Pull every quote that describes a problem, a trigger, or a decision.
2. Group them. A theme is something at least two people said unprompted.
3. Count. "Four of six", always as a fraction of the total, never a percentage. Six people is not a percentage.
4. Rank themes by count, then by how strongly it was said.

### The status quo

The status quo interview is coded separately and is exempt from the two-person rule, because it is not answering "what do customers say". It is answering "why does nothing beat us". It gets its own section in the report, written as one person's account and labelled as one. If any of the other five said something that matches, cross-reference it and say so; if not, say that too.

### Saturation check

If interviews five and six produced no theme that wasn't already there, six was enough. Say so in the output.

If they did produce new themes, six wasn't enough. Book two more and say that too.

### Unmet expectations

Open both sealed hypothesis lists. Which hypotheses never came up, in the interviews or in the sales calls from step zero?

A hypothesis nobody mentioned is usually worth more than a theme four people repeated, because it shows where the company's beliefs and the market's beliefs have come apart. Report the founder's misses and yours separately.

### The language bank

Pull the exact phrases. Not cleaned up, not shortened, not made grammatical. These go straight onto the website.

Rules: their nouns, their verbs, their profanity. If they call it a "spreadsheet problem", it's a spreadsheet problem, not a "manual process inefficiency".

The bank ships as its own file (deliverable 2), one row per phrase: the phrase verbatim (with the original-language phrase alongside where translated), the count, the evidence source (sales call, interview or public review), the speaker's role, the date. The report quotes from it; it does not replace it.

## The report

Seven sections.

1. **Interview mix** and existing material read: how many, which segment, when, interview length; how many sales calls and reviews were mined. Coverage of the nine domains and the saturation check sit here
2. **Themes**, ranked by count, with the verbatims underneath. In the app the client approves, edits or drops each theme
3. **The status quo**, one person's account of doing nothing, labelled as one
4. **Unmet expectations**, the founder's hypotheses and yours, each marked as heard or never heard
5. **Key finding**, one sentence, in a black band
6. **Language bank**, exact phrases ready for the page, with the bank as a download
7. **Limitations**

The limitations always include: six people is not a survey and no number here is a percentage; what people say they did is more reliable than what they say they want; this covers the people who talked to you, not the ones who didn't reply; and the sales calls contain only people who came to you.

## Language

Standard product marketing terms on every label and heading. Never a label built from "who, what, which, where" phrasing. Quotes stay verbatim.

| Use | Instead of |
|---|---|
| Customer interviews | Calls (sales calls stay sales calls) |
| Interview mix: recent customers, lost prospects, status quo | Bought, looked and left, did nothing |
| Hypotheses (sealed before interviews) | Your 3 guesses, sealed expectations |
| Unmet expectations | What nobody said |
| Language bank | Their words, the words to use |
| Themes | (stays) |
| Purchase trigger, economic buyer, buying process, competition | The trigger, who pays, the process, the alternatives |
| Evidence source | Where it came from |
| Key finding, Limitations | The finding, what this can't tell you |

## Voice

- Plain and direct. Their words in quotes, yours outside them.
- No slogans, no balanced two-part phrases, no line whose job is to sound clever.
- Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes, in the report or in this file.
- Never tidy a quote. Broken grammar is evidence.

## Rules

- **Three deliverables: the guide, the bank, the report.** All three on real engagements; the report alone for demo use.
- **Step zero before any booking.** Read the existing material first. Say in the report how much was read.
- **Two sealed hypothesis lists, founder's and yours,** written before anyone is booked.
- **Never quote without a count.** Every theme carries how many of six said it.
- **Never round up.** Four of six is four of six, not "most customers".
- **Never a percentage.** Six people cannot produce a percentage.
- **The model drafts, a person approves.** No theme ships without a human tracing it to the transcript.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.** The transcript is the source, not your notes and not a summary. The same applies to anything brought in from outside: a competitor's page, a review, a market claim gets fetched and read in full before it informs a theme. A search-result summary is a lead, not evidence.
- **Anonymise by default.** Role and company type, never names, unless the customer has said yes in writing.
- **If a theme has one person behind it, it isn't a theme.** Put it in the output as a single observation, marked as one. The status quo section is the one deliberate exception, and it is labelled as such.
