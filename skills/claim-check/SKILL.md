# Claim check

**A run you repeat, not a register someone maintains.** Nobody maintains a claims register by hand. What a company needs is a check that can be re-run: pull every claim from every public surface, put it beside whatever evidence stands behind it, and report the gap.

Re-run before the moments when someone else checks the claims for you: a fundraise, an enterprise procurement deal, a regulated-market launch, a press cycle. Quarterly otherwise. The output feeds the battlecards, the messaging framework, the deck and the homepage — it is where their proof point grades come from.

## What this is not

**Not Message drift.** Message drift compares the copy against the approved messaging framework — is everyone saying the agreed thing. The Claim check compares the claims against the evidence — is the agreed thing true. A company can be perfectly on-message and unable to back a word of it.

**Not legal advice.** The check uses the regulator's standard because it is the only floor that is not taste, but the output is a marketing check. Where a claim carries real regulatory exposure, the fix reads "Needs legal review".

## Why the check earns its place

- The US regulator's standing policy since 1984: an advertiser must hold a **reasonable basis for every objective claim before it ships**. "Tests prove" and "studies show" require at least that level of support. Implied claims count. Failure is by itself a deceptive practice. Fee and return claims in financial services sit in the most-watched category, and regulators keep enforcing against claims with no objective substantiation.
- A survey of 625 B2B technology buyers: **73% say most vendors fall short of honesty.** What convinces them a vendor is honest: a detailed description of how the product works (65%), candour about mistakes (63%), candid discussion of what the product cannot do (55%). Grading a claim unknown is strategy, not confession.
- Klue's audit of 150+ battlecards: **19% carry supporting evidence. Every battlecard with the highest retention carries it.** Companies claim; they do not attach.
- Compliance tools for regulated firms review one asset at a time. This check reads every surface at once, which is how it catches one claim worded three ways.

## The harvest

Read every surface in full: website, deck, app store listing, ads, one-pagers, and sales emails. Read what renders, not what sits in the markup.

Pull every claim verbatim, with its location. A claim is any statement a sceptical stranger could ask "how do you know?" about. Group the claims inventory by claim type:

| Claim type | Example |
|---|---|
| Statistic | "85% of expats have no savings" |
| Superlative or comparative | "Lowest fees in the market" |
| Uniqueness claim | "The only app that..." |
| Social proof | "Trusted by...", "200+ leads", a logo wall |
| Outcome promise | "Retire with confidence" |
| Security or regulatory claim | "Your money is protected" |
| Implied claim | The chart whose axis starts at 60; the logo of a company that never signed |

**The same claim on six surfaces is one claim with six locations.** Where two surfaces state it differently — two different fees, two different customer counts — that is a result, not a tidying job.

## Evidence grades

Same grades and fill grammar as Positioning and the Messaging framework. Each claim gets one.

| Grade | Means | Drawn as |
|---|---|---|
| `Proven` | Evidence exists, it is yours, a stranger could check it | Solid, green in the app |
| `Borrowed` | True as far as it goes, but the evidence is a benchmark, another market, or someone else's number | Half |
| `Unknown` | Nothing stands behind it | Dashed outline |
| `Contradicted` | Your own material, or a competitor's published fact, disagrees with it | Outlined, struck through |

Grade against the evidence hierarchy, strongest first: **verifiable artefact** (a thing the reader can open and inspect), **named customer outcome**, **third-party validation**, **self-assertion**. Self-assertion is not proof; it is the claim restated. A stale artefact — the churned customer's logo, the case study for a workflow no longer sold — grades Contradicted, not Proven, because a buyer who catches one stale proof discounts all of it.

**If most claims grade Proven, check your standard.** An early-stage company's claims are mostly unknown, and saying so is the honest result.

## High-risk claims

Rank by three factors multiplied, not averaged:

1. **Claim scope.** A sweeping claim on weak evidence reads as evasion; the gap itself becomes the message.
2. **Evidence strength.** Graded as above.
3. **Surface exposure.** A claim in the deck gets checked in diligence. A fee claim on the homepage gets checked by a regulator and by every competitor. A claim in a blog post from 2023 mostly doesn't.

Comparative superlatives about money — cheapest, lowest, best returns — go to the top whenever they are not Proven, in any regulated industry. The client sets the industry once (regulated financial services, regulated health, not regulated).

## The fixes

For every high-risk claim there are three honest moves, and the output writes one out:

**Strengthen the proof.** Name the specific evidence that would close the gap and the cheapest way to get it: name the customer, date the benchmark, publish the methodology, show the fee table beside the competitors'.

**Narrow the claim.** Chronically underrated. A modest claim with strong evidence builds more trust than a grand claim with none. For the top high-risk claims, write the narrowed version out in full — before and after — so the founder can ship it the same day.

**Remove the claim.** Narrowing it to zero. Sometimes right.

Where the claim carries regulatory exposure, the fix is **Needs legal review**, with an interim narrowed claim the client may use until the lawyer answers.

## The report's structure

Header: `Claim check · N claims · N proven · <surfaces read> · <date>`

**01 · Claims inventory** — every claim drawn as a mark, grouped by claim type, each in its grade, with the tally. The picture is the point: how much of what the company says has no evidence, visible in one look.

**02 · High-risk claims** — ranked, each with its locations, grade, surface exposure, and the reason in one plain sentence.

**03 · Claim trace** — the top high-risk claim on every surface it appears on, quoted verbatim from each, with the evidence that exists beside it. Where surfaces disagree, show both.

**04 · Fixes** — for the top claims, the move chosen and the rewrite or the evidence to collect.

**05 · Key finding** — one sentence, black band. About the claims, not the method. "Your homepage leads with a fee claim a competitor's published fee contradicts" is a key finding. "Most claims are unknown" is a summary.

**06 · Limitations** — the standing limits, including that this reads public surfaces and uploaded files only, and that a claim graded Proven today decays.

The deliverable adds the claims register (a CSV of every claim with locations, grade, evidence and fix) and the re-run triggers.

## Drawing rules

- Claims as marks, grouped by claim type, in fill grammar. Never a table where a drawing works.
- The claim trace drawn as a row of surfaces with the quote under each and the evidence in the last panel.
- Grades in fill. Green only on Proven and on fixes marked live.
- Never a percentage on fewer than thirty claims. Nine of forty-one is nine of forty-one.

## Language

Standard product marketing terms on every label and heading. Never a label built from "who, what, which, where" phrasing. Claims themselves are always quoted verbatim.

| Use | Instead of |
|---|---|
| Claims inventory | What you say, claim corpus |
| Claim types: statistic, superlative or comparative, uniqueness claim, social proof, outcome promise, security or regulatory claim, implied claim | Kinds |
| Evidence grades: Proven, Borrowed, Unknown, Contradicted | Unchecked, running on faith, your own site disagrees |
| Evidence hierarchy | (stays) |
| High-risk claims, ranked by claim scope, evidence strength, surface exposure | The dangerous ones |
| Claim trace | One claim, traced |
| Strengthen the proof, Narrow the claim, Remove the claim | Raise the proof, shrink the claim, de-risk the messaging |
| Needs legal review | Ask a lawyer, legal review recommended |
| Claims register | (the CSV download) |
| Re-run triggers | (stays) |
| Key finding, Limitations | The finding, what can't be known |

## Standing rules for every output

- **A run you repeat.** The value of the method is that the run repeats. State the re-run triggers in the deliverable.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.** Every surface fetched and read in full, rendered not markup. Every piece of evidence opened before it counts as proof.
- **Quote claims verbatim.** Never paraphrase the claim being checked.
- **No invented findings. No false zeroes.** Where evidence exists but is weak, say what exists.
- **Never soften a grade.** Unknown is the normal early-stage state and the report says so without apology.
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
- **Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes. No balanced two-part lines.** If a line would work on a poster, cut it.
