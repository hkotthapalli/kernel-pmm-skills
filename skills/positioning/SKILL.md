# Positioning

**Positioning is a choice about which comparison you want buyers to judge you against.**

Get it right and everything downstream is easier. Get it wrong and every page, deck and campaign argues against a comparison you never chose.

This output states that choice, shows the evidence under each part of it, compares two or three positioning options rather than one, says what would prove it wrong, and ends in homepage copy that expresses it.

## Two deliverables

**1. The report.** Sections 01 to 08 below: the competition, the differentiators, the target customer, the options, the evidence grades, the validation test and the homepage.

**2. The homepage copy document.** Section 07's content in its own file: the zone list with the final copy for each zone, nothing else. No evidence, no options, no grades. The person who builds the page (a developer, a site-builder prompt, a contractor) should never receive a strategy report and be asked to find the copy inside it.

The report is the decision. The copy document is the handover. On a real engagement both ship. For demo or portfolio use, the report alone is enough.

## What this is not

**Not a review of the current page.** That is the Landing page review, which judges what exists and stops at the first failure. This output says what the new page has to say. If this document starts grading the existing site, stop and hand it over. The one exception: a claim on the current site that the evidence contradicts is listed as a claim to remove, with the reason.

**Not a slogan.** No taglines, no headline candidates in the strategy sections. Copy appears once, in the homepage section, and only after an option is chosen.

**Not category creation by default.** A new category is one of three category approaches and the most expensive. See below.

**Not the messaging framework.** Positioning decides the comparison. The messaging framework works out the message for each buying role.

## Inputs, and gaps

Positioning is assembled from work already done, not generated fresh:

| Input | Comes from |
|---|---|
| Competition, including the status quo | Voice of customer and Win / loss, counted |
| Competitors | The Fifty, including the status quo column |
| Differentiators | The Table-stakes read, which separates table stakes from differentiator candidates, with buyer validation |
| Target customer | ICP and segments |
| Proof per claim | Claim check |
| Why now | The Why now output. Reference it, do not rebuild it here |

**Where an input does not exist, say so and grade that part unknown.** Do not fill a gap from imagination. A positioning document with visible holes is worth more than a complete one that is half invented.

**One hard floor: no positioning with zero customer interviews.** The founder knows the market better than anyone, but does not know which comparison the buyer made, and that is the whole question. If nobody has spoken to a buyer, run the Voice of customer interviews first. With fewer than six, label the result a hypothesis at the top of the page.

## The five parts

Standard product marketing terms, in April Dunford's order.

| Part | The question it settles |
|---|---|
| **Competition** | If you vanished tomorrow, what would buyers do? Including the status quo |
| **Differentiator** | What you have that the competition doesn't |
| **Value** | What changes for the buyer because of it, with proof |
| **Target customer** | The buyers who care about that value most |
| **Category** | The market context that makes the differentiator obvious |

The order runs upward. The competition decides what counts as a differentiator. The differentiator decides the value you can claim. The value decides the target customer. The target customer decides the category. **Starting with the category is the most common way this goes wrong.** It produces a category chosen because it sounds good, then a scramble to justify it.

### Choosing the value

The hardest step. A company may have many differentiators, but buyers hold one idea, two at most, three as a ceiling. The user picks one to three differentiators to lead with. Each shows the buyer quote behind it and its evidence mark. Anything without buyer validation cannot be picked and goes to the Assumption log to test first.

### Prefer what is hard to copy

Ask of every differentiator: how long would it take a funded competitor to match this, and what would they have to give up to do it? If the answer is "a quarter, and nothing", it belongs on the product roadmap, not in the positioning. Show the estimate as "time for a competitor to copy", and let the user change it.

## Two or three positioning options, one recommended

Produce two or three options. Recommend one. More than three is a menu. Fewer than two means you have not looked.

Each option carries:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category approach** | Existing category head to head, subcategory of an existing category, or new category |
| **Category** | The named category |
| **Target customer** | The segment it is for |
| **Main competition** | The current solution or status quo |
| **Differentiator** | What only you have |
| **Evidence** | The grade of each claim, drawn as marks |
| **Cost of being wrong** | What you would have to undo: pages, pricing, roadmap, hires |
| **Not a fit** | The buyers and deals this option turns away |

**"Not a fit" is never empty.** An option that excludes nobody is a description, not a position.

Draw the options in one table, rows aligned, so each row compares across options. The recommendation gets a one-sentence reason drawn from the evidence.

## The three category approaches

| Approach | What it means | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing category, head to head** | Compete in a category buyers already understand | You inherit the category's buying criteria, including the ones you lose on |
| **Subcategory of an existing category** | A defensible part of an understood category | Your ceiling is the size of the subcategory, and the leader can follow you into it |
| **New category** | Name and teach a new category | You pay to educate the market. The published playbook assumes a coordinated launch across every function, which a seed company cannot fund |

State the approach chosen and the cost accepted. **Never present a new category as the ambitious choice without pricing it.**

## Evidence grades

Every claim in the position carries a grade, drawn in the same fill grammar as every other output.

| Grade | Means | Drawn as |
|---|---|---|
| `Proven` | Evidence exists, it is yours, a stranger could check it | Solid, green in the app |
| `Borrowed` | True, but the evidence is an industry benchmark, another market, or a competitor's number | Half |
| `Unknown` | Nobody has tested it | Dashed outline |
| `Contradicted` | You claim it and something of yours disagrees | Outlined, struck through |

A reader should see at a glance how much of the position is made up. Show a tally of the grades. **If most of it is proven, check your standard.** Early-stage positioning is mostly unknown, and saying so is the honest result.

## The validation test

Written before the position ships, never after. Two things, both required.

**The test.** A number or an observable event that would mean this option was wrong, checkable by someone who was not in the room, with a date. "Messaging doesn't resonate" is not a test. "Option A is wrong if fewer than 3 of the next 20 sign-ups say they plan to retire outside the UAE, by 31 January" is. In the app, the test is one sentence with three editable values, and Kernel counts against it.

**Review trigger.** The event that makes the position out of date rather than wrong. Usually one of: a competitor claims the same thing, the main competition disappears, the target customer stops being reachable, or the differentiator becomes table stakes. Name it and hand it to the Competitor monitor.

A position with no way to be wrong is a belief.

## The homepage

The position is internal. The homepage is how a stranger experiences it. **Never put the positioning statement on the website.** It is written for the team, and buyers do not read in that shape.

The homepage section carries:

- The page as zones, top to bottom (hero, competition, proof, how it works, close)
- Against each zone, the part of the position it expresses, with its mark
- Production-ready copy, not direction. "Lead with the outcome" is not copy
- The headline called out in a black band, with its evidence source

Copy rules: use the buyer's words from the language bank, never invented phrasing. Name the competition where the research supports it. Buyers already compare, and leaving the competitor unnamed does not stop them. Keep the page arguing the chosen option only. A homepage hedging across two options is the failure this output exists to prevent.

## The report's structure

Header: `Positioning · N options · N claims unknown · <sources> · <date>`

**01 · Competition** — counted, drawn as marks, including the status quo.

**02 · Differentiators** — each against the competition, with its evidence mark and time to copy.

**03 · Target customer** — the target customer and who is not a fit.

**04 · Positioning options** — two or three in one table, with the recommendation in one sentence.

**05 · Your positioning** — the five parts, each with its evidence source and grade, the tally, and any claim to remove.

**06 · Validation test** — the test with its number and date, and the review trigger.

**07 · Homepage copy** — zones mapped to the position, copy, headline.

**08 · Limitations.**

## Drawing rules

- Counts as marks, filled out of the total. The reader counts.
- Options in one aligned table, never stacked as prose.
- Grades in fill. Green only on Proven.
- The homepage drawn as blocks, in the same grammar as the Landing page review.

## Language

Standard product marketing terms on every label and heading. Never invented or game-like words, and never a label built from "who, what, which, where" phrasing.

| Use | Instead of |
|---|---|
| Competition | Alternatives, substitutes, what they'd do instead |
| Status quo | Doing nothing, null alternative |
| Differentiator | USP, moat, what only you have |
| Value | Why that matters |
| Target customer | Who cares most |
| Category | Shelf, frame of reference |
| Positioning option | Bet, play, candidate |
| Category approach: existing category, subcategory, new category | A corner of the market, a new shelf, the market that exists |
| Not a fit | Not for, anti-ICP |
| Validation test, review trigger | Kill criterion, losing number, expiry condition |
| Cost of being wrong | Rework if wrong, cost of the bet |
| Evidence source | Where it came from |
| Key finding, Limitations | The finding, what this can't tell you |

Never write "positioning statement" more than once outside the title. Never use "category king", "magnetic", "north star" or "single source of truth".

## Voice

- Plain and direct. Their words in quotes, yours outside them.
- No slogans, no balanced two-part phrases, no line whose job is to sound clever. If a line has a rhythm, a turn, or would work on a poster, cut it.
- The recommendation is a fact and a reason, not a flourish.
- Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes.

## Rules

- **Two deliverables: the report and the homepage copy document.**
- **Build upward from the competition.** Never start at the category.
- **The competition is counted, not asserted.** Every entry traces to an interview or a deal, and the status quo is always a row.
- **Two or three options, one recommended.**
- **Every option names who is not a fit.**
- **Every claim carries a grade.** Unknown is a legitimate and common answer.
- **A validation test with a number and a date, written before shipping.**
- **Price the category approach.** Especially a new category.
- **Hard to copy beats absent today.**
- **The positioning stays internal.** The homepage is the external form.
- **Never review the existing page here**, apart from listing contradicted claims to remove.
- **Never a percentage under thirty.** Four of twelve is four of twelve.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.** Competitor claims get read on their own pages in full.
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
