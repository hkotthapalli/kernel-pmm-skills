# Deck spine

**The spine is the argument. The slides are how it gets carried.**

Most decks are assembled as a list of expected slides and then a story is
retrofitted onto them. This output does it the other way: the argument first, in
order, then the slides that each carry one step of it, then what it would take to
fill the slides you cannot yet build.

**Two deliverables, always:**

1. **The deck** — a real, built presentation file.
2. **The report** — the spine, the slide-by-slide brief, the claims graded, the
   finding, and what can't be known.

Neither substitutes for the other. The deck is what gets presented. The report is
what tells the founder whether it makes the case, and it is the artefact that
survives after the deck is redesigned by somebody else.

## What this is not

Not a design job. Nothing here is about typeface, colour or layout beyond what
carries the argument.

Not the strategic narrative. That is the company-level point of view and it feeds
this. The spine is one argument aimed at one room on one occasion.

Not the positioning. Positioning decides which comparison you are judged against.
The spine assumes that decision is already made.

## How long you actually have

Research on thousands of seed decks puts the average investor review at **three
minutes forty-four seconds**, and only **58% of decks are viewed to the last
slide**.

Both numbers are design constraints, not trivia.

- **You are allocating 224 seconds.** Every slide has a share of it, and the
  report says what that share is.
- **Nearly half never reach the end.** The case has to be makeable from the first
  eight slides. If the argument only closes on slide fifteen, four in ten readers
  never see it close.

The same research gives average seconds per section. Use it to decide where the
effort goes:

| Section | Seconds |
|---|---|
| Business model | 64 |
| Product | 59 |
| Traction | 40 |
| Team | 38 |
| Financials | 37 |
| Problem · Solution · Competition | 34 each |
| The ask | 32 |
| Market size | 29 |
| Purpose | 26 |
| Why now | 23 |

**The business model gets the longest look of anything in the deck**, and most
guides give it a line. That single fact should change where the work goes.

One more, and it reads backwards: investors spend around **80% more time on the
traction of companies that failed to raise**. Extra time on traction is scrutiny,
not interest. If your traction needs studying, it is not doing its job.

## The opening, decided

There is a real disagreement here and the report has to take a side rather than
list both.

The conventional structure opens with the problem. The best-known narrative
practitioner says the opposite — that asserting someone has a problem puts them
on the defensive, and you should open with an undeniable shift in the world
instead.

**Both are right, for different rooms. The test is who in the room has the
problem.**

| Who you are pitching | Open with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The person who has the problem | The shift | They get defensive about admitting it, and a shift invites them to talk instead |
| Someone judging whether others have it | The problem | An investor does not have your customer's problem. Naming it directly costs nothing |

For an investor deck that means problem-first is correct — **but the shift still
has to appear by slide four**, or there is no answer to why this is happening
now. Take the why-now from that output; do not rebuild it here.

## The spine

Before any slide exists, write the argument as a numbered list of one-line steps.
Six to nine steps. Each step is a claim, not a topic.

"Market size" is a topic. "The money already exists and nobody invests it" is a
step.

Two tests:

**The retelling test.** Could someone who sat through this repeat the argument to
a colleague the next day? They will have to. Most decks fail here because the
argument was never stated, only implied across twelve slides.

**The removal test.** Take any step out. If the argument still stands, the step
was decoration and the slide carrying it can go.

## The slides

One slide per step, plus the fixed furniture — cover, team, ask, contact.

Each slide carries:

| | |
|---|---|
| **The line** | The actual sentence on the slide. Not a description of it |
| **What it shows** | The specific evidence, chart or screen |
| **Seconds** | Its share of the 224 |
| **State** | Whether it can be built today |

**The line is written, not described.** "Show market opportunity" is not a line.
If you cannot write the sentence, the slide is not ready and it goes in the
missing list.

**One idea per slide, and the audience either reads or listens.** A slide dense
enough to read is a slide nobody hears you over.

### Slide states

| State | Means | Drawn as |
|---|---|---|
| `Ready` | The line is written and the evidence exists | Solid |
| `Thin` | Buildable, but the evidence is borrowed or from another market | Half |
| `Can't build` | The evidence does not exist yet | Dashed outline |
| `Contradicted` | You have it and something of yours disagrees | Outlined, struck through |

## Building the deck

Build the file after the report, never before. Read the presentation-format skill
for the mechanics.

Rules for what goes in it:

- **Ready and Thin slides get built.** Thin slides carry their borrowed source
  visibly — an industry figure is labelled as one.
- **Can't-build slides are built as placeholders that state what is missing.**
  A slide reading "Traction: we need the lesson-one completion number" is
  uncomfortable and correct. **Never fabricate a number to fill a hole.** The
  founder decides whether to cut the slide or go and get the evidence, and they
  can only decide that if they can see it.
- **Contradicted slides do not get built at all** until the contradiction is
  resolved. Building both sides of a contradiction into a deck is how a founder
  gets caught in a meeting.
- Speaker notes carry the seconds budget for that slide and the source for every
  figure on it.
- The last slide is the ask and the contact, even though four in ten will not
  reach it. It still has to be there.

## Every claim gets graded

Pull every factual claim out of the deck and out of the elevator pitch, and grade
it.

| State | Means |
|---|---|
| `Sourced` | You can name where it came from and a stranger could check it |
| `Borrowed` | True, but from another market, another year, or someone else's report |
| `Unsourced` | Stated with confidence and nobody knows where it came from |
| `Contradicted` | Another of your own documents disagrees |

**Percentages in an elevator pitch are the highest-risk claims in the company.**
They get repeated in every meeting, they end up in the press, and the first
investor who checks one and finds nothing behind it stops believing the rest.

## The report

Header: `Deck spine · N slides · N can't be built · <sources> · <date>`

**01 · The deck** — every slide as a glyph in running order, named, drawn in its
state, with the total time budget.

**02 · The spine** — the argument as numbered steps, one line each.

**03 · Slide by slide** — the line, what it shows, its seconds, its state.

**04 · Where the claims stand** — every number graded, with the ones that need a
source named.

**05 · The finding** — one sentence, black band.

**06 · What can't be known.**

Six sections.

### Drawing the deck in section 01

**The deck appears at the top of the report as a row of glyphs**, one per slide,
in running order, each named beneath it and drawn in its state.

Each glyph is a slide-shaped rectangle carrying a mark that suggests its content —
a bar for a claim, stacked bars for a comparison, a filled block for a screen,
rising steps for growth, small marks for people, a single heavy bar for the ask.
The reader sees the shape of the whole deck, and which parts of it are hollow,
before reading a word.

This is the same move as the competitive grid: the drawing carries the finding.

## The finding

The founder opened this to answer one question: **does this deck make the case,
and if not, where does it fail.**

It is about the argument or the evidence behind it. Not about design, not about
what investors generally like, not about the framework.

Test it: could the founder read that line and know what to change before the next
meeting? Findings that pass tend to be of the form:

- The slide investors look at longest is the one you have not written.
- The number your whole pitch rests on has no source.
- Four in ten readers stop before your argument closes.

## Voice

- Plain and direct. The lines on the slides are written as they would be spoken.
- No slogans, no balanced two-part phrases, no line whose job is to sound clever.
- Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes.
- Never write a slide line you would be embarrassed to say out loud.

## Language

| Use | Never |
|---|---|
| The argument | The narrative arc, the story spine |
| The line | Headline copy, slide message |
| Can't build | Gap, TBD, placeholder |
| The shift | Macro trend, tailwind, paradigm |

## Rules

- **Two deliverables. The deck and the report.**
- **Spine before slides. Report before deck.**
- **Six to nine steps.** Fewer and there is no argument, more and nobody can
  retell it.
- **The case closes by slide eight.** Four in ten never see the rest.
- **Write the line, don't describe the slide.**
- **Budget the seconds.** All 224 of them.
- **Put the work where the eyes are.** The business model gets the longest look.
- **Traction that needs studying has already failed.**
- **Every number graded, especially the ones in the elevator pitch.**
- **The shift appears by slide four**, wherever you open.
- **One idea per slide.** They read it or they hear you, never both.
- **Never invent traction.** A slide you cannot fill is built as a stated hole.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.** Any figure carried into the deck gets
  traced to the document it came from, and dated.
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
