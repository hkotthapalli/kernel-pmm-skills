# ICP and segments

Four jobs in one document: the segments, the best-fit segment, the disqualifiers, and the beachhead segment.

## Two deliverables

**1. The working set.** Two small files on the client's own drive. First, the scored segment sheet — segments down, the five criteria across, each cell holding the mark and the evidence line, so the scoring can be redone when new customers arrive. Second, a one-page disqualification sheet written for live use: the groups not to chase, one plain line each, with the sentence you will hear on a call, phrased so a founder or first salesperson can recognise a bad-fit conversation in the first five minutes and end it politely. The disqualification sheet is the piece that changes behaviour. The segment sheet keeps the choice honest over time.

**2. The report.** The segments drawn, scored and judged, the beachhead segment named, the key finding.

On a real engagement both ship. For demo or portfolio use, the report alone is enough.

## Where it comes from

**Normally: best-fit customers you won and kept.** Not the ones you won and lost, and never the ones you wish you had. Look at who renewed, who expanded, who referred someone. This is April Dunford's first step too: list your best-fit customers.

**At seed stage you do not have enough of those.** Sixty beta users and one signed company is not a dataset. So the ICP leans on the research instead — the customer interviews, the win-loss, the losses in particular. This is the honest version and it is worth saying out loud, because the alternative on offer elsewhere is a workshop where the founder describes their imagined buyer and everyone writes it down.

One competitor states on their own site that they do not interview customers, because it would make them slower and more expensive and they do not know the client's industry. Another offers "ICP definition with evidence (interviews **or synthesis**)". Synthesis means somebody guessed, neatly. Name the evidence source of every segment so the reader can tell which kind they are holding.

**How the customer evidence gets in, in order of preference:**

1. **A typed best-fit customer list.** Five to ten customers, each ticked: retained 3+ months, expanded, referred, fast conversion. Then three to five churned or lost, with the reason in their words. For B2B, the user types the website and Kernel fills size, industry and stage from it. Initials are fine for people. An optional line per customer records the purchase trigger. This is the default because it takes ten minutes and needs no tools.
2. **A file.** A CSV from Stripe, the app's user table, a CRM export or a spreadsheet.
3. **A CRM connection** (HubSpot, Attio, Pipedrive, Salesforce). Won-deal analysis needs roughly fifty closed deals before the patterns hold, so most early companies will not have enough.
4. **Voice of customer interviews**, always. Below about twenty customers they carry most of the weight.

Every segment shows its evidence from each source separately: interviews as one row of marks, best-fit customers as another.

**Buyer type decides what a segment is.** Ask once per workspace: B2B (a company pays), B2C (a person pays), or both. For B2B, a segment is a group of companies defined by a purchase trigger (such as "just hired its first salesperson"), with size and industry beside it. For B2C, a segment is a group of people defined by what they do. Buying roles and personas inside a company belong to the Buying committee output, which takes the beachhead segment from here. For B2C, the Buying committee maps household roles: co-decision maker, influencer, incumbent owner, regulator.

## Reachability, the test that kills most segments

**Can you build a list of these people by Friday?**

If you cannot name a list or channel that reaches them, it is not a segment. It is a description of a mood.

Every segment gets a reachability line, and it has to name a place: an employer, a register, a community, a job title on a platform, a company list. "Expats aged 35 to 50" names no place. "Staff at GCC employers who run an end-of-service scheme" names one. Kernel suggests a place, the user accepts, edits or replaces it.

Segments that fail this test still go in the document. They are drawn failing it, because a founder needs to see that the segment they love cannot be reached.

## Behaviour, not demographics

For a consumer product especially, a segment is something people **do**, not something they **are**.

| Segment | Description |
|---|---|
| Meant to start and never did | People aged 35 to 50 |
| Already pays somebody for advice | Affluent professionals |
| Thinks the end-of-service payout is their pension | Expat employees |

The behaviour cuts across the demographics, and it is the thing you can write copy against. Age bands are for market sizing, not for targeting. Segment names stay behavioural and in buyers' words.

**Where the client's own documents disagree** (an age band, a target segment, across a deck, an app store listing and a market model), say so and do not quietly pick one. List each disagreement after the scored grid and keep both until the client picks one.

## Scoring

Five criteria, no more. Four come from Geoffrey Moore's beachhead test and one is reachability.

| Criterion | Passes when |
|---|---|
| **Reachable** | You can name a list or channel that reaches them |
| **Market size** | It could carry the plan on its own |
| **Winnable** | You could be the obvious choice in it, not the ninth option |
| **Product fit** | Your strongest differentiator is the thing this segment needs |
| **Reference customer** | At least one customer in it who would go on record |

Score with the fill grammar: solid passes, half-filled partly, dashed fails. A short reason sits under each mark. Never a number out of ten — a score implies precision that is not there, and it lets people average away a fatal zero. A count of passes beside each row is fine.

## Disqualifiers

Every ICP needs its opposite. List the segments you will not chase and why, in one line each, with the sentence you will hear on a call and the evidence source.

The most useful disqualifiers are the ones that look like customers: people who have already solved the problem another way, people the product is not built for, and any segment whose inclusion contradicts the company's own founding premise. That last one is common and nobody catches it.

This section is also the source of the disqualification sheet in the working set. The report states the list. The sheet formats it for a live conversation.

## The beachhead segment

Name it, and name the next segment it opens — Moore's bowling alley. The beachhead segment is chosen partly because of which segment it makes easier to enter afterwards.

State the reference customer by role, not by name, unless permission exists in writing.

## The report's structure

Header: `ICP and segments · N segments · <date>`.

**01 · Segments** — each with the behaviour that defines it, its reachability, and the evidence with a count per source.

**02 · Scored** — the segments against the five criteria, drawn as a grid of marks. Sorted by passes. Then any disagreements between the client's own documents.

**03 · Beachhead segment and disqualifiers** — the beachhead segment with its reasoning and the next segment, then the disqualifiers.

**04 · Key finding** — one sentence, black band.

**05 · Limitations** — the standing limits.

## Drawing rules

- The scored grid is the main visual. Segments down, criteria across, one mark per cell.
- Draw the segments that fail reachability the same size as the ones that pass. Do not shrink them.
- No numbers out of ten, no percentages on a base under thirty.
- Fill grammar first. Green marks passes with evidence, counts that are direct evidence, reachability that names a real list, and the beachhead segment.

## Writing rules

- No section subtitles unless they carry something the drawing does not.
- Never a balanced two-part line.
- The key finding is a fact, not a formulation.

## Language

Standard product marketing terms on every label and heading. Never a label built from "who, what, which, where" phrasing.

| Use | Instead of |
|---|---|
| Ideal customer profile (ICP) | Who we sell to |
| Best-fit customers | Your best customers |
| Retained 3+ months, Expanded, Referred, Fast conversion | Still paying, paid more, referred someone, bought quickly |
| Churned or lost | Customers who left, people who didn't buy |
| Buyer type: B2B, B2C, both | Who pays you |
| Purchase trigger | What was happening when they bought |
| Reachability | Where you'd find them |
| Reachable, Market size, Winnable, Product fit, Reference customer | Can you build a list, Big enough to matter, Small enough to lead, Fits what you're good at, Someone who will talk |
| Beachhead segment | The one to start with |
| Next segment | What it opens next |
| Disqualifiers, disqualification sheet | Who to say no to, say-no page |
| Customer interviews | Calls |
| Evidence source | How Kernel knows |
| Key finding, Limitations | The finding, what can't be known |

## Standing rules for every output

- **Two deliverables: the working set and the report.** The working set on real engagements always.
- **No invented findings.** Every segment traces to a named piece of research with a count.
- **No false zeroes.**
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
- **"Not measured" is not an answer.**
- **Read the source, never the snippet.**
- **Where the client's own documents contradict each other, put it in the output.** Two different age bands across a deck and a market model is a finding, not a tidying job.
