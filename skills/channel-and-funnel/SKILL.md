# Channel and funnel

Distribution, not product, is the number one cause of startup failure, and most companies get zero channels to work. This output decides which ways of finding customers the business can afford, proves them with cheap tests, and draws the funnel each one feeds. It sells the decision, which nobody else sells — the market sells channel execution.

## Two deliverables

**1. The channel test plan.** A working file on the client's own drive: the three tests, each with its pass number, date, budget cap and owner, and a tally column. Test bounds are fixed: roughly a thousand dollars and a month per test, often less.

**2. The report.** The affordability picture, the menu, the tests, the funnel per route, the finding.

On a real engagement both ship; for demo or portfolio use, the report alone is enough.

## First: what a customer pays decides how you may find them

One plain idea opens the output: **what one customer pays you in a year decides how much you can spend to find one.**

A customer who pays $60 a year cannot be won with a $200 ad — that loses money on every customer, forever. At that price only ways of being found that cost nearly nothing can work: people telling each other, a community, content found on its own, or being handed over by someone else (an employer, a platform, a partner). A customer who pays $50,000 a year can afford a salesperson spending months on them.

The middle is the **danger zone**: too expensive for a casual click to convert, too cheap to fund a salesperson. Companies stuck there run a patchwork of half-channels and lose money on every path.

Two rules inside this:

- **Count a year of revenue, not a lifetime.** If it takes more than a year to earn back what a customer cost to win, growth eats cash the company does not have.
- **Place each route to purchase separately, never merged.** A consumer tier and an employer tier are two placements, usually far apart — and the same product can be one route's market and the other route's channel.

**The standing chain: any pricing change re-runs this output.** What you charge and how you find customers are never decided in silos.

## Then: the Bullseye discipline on the affordable menu

1. **The outer ring: everything.** One idea for every plausible channel on the affordable menu, no dismissals — the ring exists to counteract bias, and the useful ideas usually arrive after the obvious ones.
2. **The middle ring: three tests.** Promote the two or three with a real drop-off in excitement after them. Run in parallel. Each test answers exactly three questions: what does a customer cost through this channel, how many customers are available through it, and are they the customers you want right now.
3. **Pass numbers before the tests.** Each test carries a number and a date written before it runs, checkable by someone who was not in the room. Testing is not scaling: four ads, not forty.
4. **The centre: one dominant engine, with named feeders.** The one-channel rule and the layered-engines school are both right, and the resolution is structural: one engine dominates, other channels exist only to feed it, and the report names which feeds what. A patchwork of equals is the danger zone's symptom wearing a strategy's clothes.
5. **The stop-list.** Channels that somewhat worked in testing are distractions, and they get killed in writing. This is the discipline founders skip.
6. **If no channel passes, the bucket leaks.** Repeat the process after looking at the product and the message, not before.

**Evidence first**: where the existing users and leads actually came from is the opening question, and if nobody recorded it, that is a marked gap in the report.

## Capture and memory

At any moment roughly 95% of buyers are not in market (the Ehrenberg-Bass 95:5 finding). Every channel row is tagged:

- **Capture** — reaches the few deciding now. Search, the benefits-enrolment moment, a referral at the point of pain.
- **Memory** — builds presence with the many who will decide later. Community, content, brand.

A company whose research shows buyers "meaning to start for years" is looking at the out-of-market state directly; its memory channels are not wasted spend, they are the queue. But a plan made only of memory channels has no revenue date, and the report says which is which.

## The funnel, per route

Five stages in plain words: **found you · started · kept going · brought someone · paid.** One funnel per route, drawn to scale like the market report's bars — the drop is the argument. Honest numbers where they exist, dashes where nothing is measured.

- **Started** (activation) is owned by the Onboarding journey output; this funnel consumes its activation moment, never redefines it.
- **Brought someone** (referral) is only a stage if word-of-mouth is affordable and real.
- On a sales route, **win rate beats lead volume**: the number of conversations needed is the target divided by the win rate, and improving qualification moves it more than adding leads.

## The report's structure

Header: `Channel and funnel · N routes · N tests planned · <date>`.

**01 · What a customer pays decides how you may find them** — the band drawn, each route placed, the danger zone shaded. Left end labelled "a customer pays you little: only free ways of being found"; right end "a customer pays you a lot: salespeople are affordable". The caption states the arithmetic in one sentence: at $X a year, an ad costing $Y per customer loses money forever.

**02 · The rings** — the outer ring at count scale, the three promoted tests with their pass numbers and dates, capture or memory tagged per row.

**03 · The engine and its feeders** — the dominant engine named, feeders drawn pointing into it, the stop-list struck through.

**04 · The funnel per route** — the bars, to scale, dashes where unmeasured.

**05 · The finding** — one sentence, black band.

**06 · What can't be known** — standing limits, always including: a pass number is a bet; channel economics decay as channels saturate; and where nobody recorded where existing users came from, the first test is finding out.

## Drawing rules

- The affordability band as one horizontal strip, routes as marks on it, danger zone shaded.
- Rings as concentric counts, not a channel essay.
- Feeder arrows into one engine; the stop-list in strikethrough.
- Funnels as scaled bars, never tables. Counts, never percentages under thirty.

## Language

Written for a cold reader. The two most common words in this discipline are banned:

| Use | Never |
|---|---|
| What one customer pays you in a year | ARPU, average revenue per user, ACV |
| What it costs to win one customer | CAC, acquisition cost |
| What the business can afford | Channel-model fit |
| The deciding few and the waiting many | In-market and out-of-market segments |
| One engine, with feeders | Multi-channel strategy, omnichannel |
| The stop-list | Deprioritised channels |
| Found you, started, kept going, brought someone, paid | Acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue |

## Standing rules for every output

- **Two deliverables: the test plan and the report.**
- **What a customer pays decides the menu, per route, never merged.**
- **Pass numbers before tests. Testing is not scaling.**
- **One engine, named feeders, a written stop-list.**
- **No invented findings. No false zeroes.** An unrecorded lead source is a gap, not a guess.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.**
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
- **Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes. No balanced two-part lines.**
