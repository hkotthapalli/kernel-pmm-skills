# Product Oracle

The Oracle is the document everyone else's document is built from. Positioning, battlecards, playbooks, launch decks, landing pages and support answers all trace back to one canonical statement of what the product is, what it does, what it costs and who it is for.

Its value comes entirely from being **short and singular**: two pages, one product, one owner, dated on page one. The market's alternatives are a 35-section brand bible nobody opens twice or a knowledge base that is a pile, not a document. A twelve-page Oracle is a wiki, and a wiki is where alignment goes to be ignored.

## Two readers

The Oracle now serves two readers, and is written for both:

- **The person**, who reads it in fragments under time pressure. Scannable blocks, numbered sections, no prose walls.
- **The machine.** The Oracle is the file the company's AI tools read first — before drafting an email, a deck, an answer. Quantified, attributable, superlative-free text is what a model can repeat without inventing. This is stated in the document as a purpose: point every internal AI at the Oracle, and everything drafted downstream starts from truth.

## Deliverable shape

The Oracle **is** the artefact — the one output where the working document is the whole point. On an engagement it ships as the two-pager plus a short report judging its field states (filled from evidence, filled from someone's word, TBD). For demo use, the miniature Oracle with its states shown is the report.

## Structure — six sections, two pages

### 1. The basics
Name · internal name · release status and date · what release material exists. Then the **Overview**: four to six short lines answering what is it, what makes it different, why believe that. Each line is a claim a founder could say aloud without qualification — and **each line carries its grade from the Claim check** in fill grammar. The canonical statements must not quietly quote faith.

### 2. How it works, quantified
Delivery, mechanics, limits. "Real-time" is not a specification; "2-minute update, 100 requests a minute" is. For apps: core workflows, permissions, integrations. Link documentation rather than becoming it.

### 3. Experience, metrics and limits
Four short blocks: what a customer's day looks like with it (2 to 3 lines) · usage metrics (how you would know it is used) · market metrics (how you would know it is working commercially) · **limitations and prerequisites, never blank**. An Oracle with no limitations section is a brochure, and the limits get discovered in front of a prospect instead.

### 4. Value proposition and packaging
The primary positioning line (the canonical sentence everything else quotes) · the supporting line · trial or freemium status · what plan it lives in · **pricing as a pointer to the pricing document, never a copied number**. Duplicated numbers are guaranteed to diverge.

### 5. Who it is for — best fit, good fit, anti-persona
Three columns with types and named examples where permission exists. The **anti-persona is mandatory and most often omitted**: who this is not for and why. It stops sales burning cycles on deals that churn, and it is the clearest signal the document was written with judgment. If the team resists naming one, that resistance is a finding: positioning has not been decided, and the Oracle says so rather than papering over it.

### 6. Competitive context
Two or three lines: the main alternatives and the one structural reason to choose this. Link the battlecard; battlecards change faster than Oracles.

## Guardrails

- **Never invent metrics, limits or customers.** `TBD` and `[Needs input: who has this]` are legitimate outputs. A confidently wrong Oracle propagates its error into every downstream asset.
- Every quantified claim traceable to a spec, a document or a named person.
- **No superlatives without proof.** "The largest" requires a number and a comparison, or it does not appear.
- Two pages. If it will not fit, either the product needs two Oracles or section 2 has become documentation. Link instead.
- Owner and last-updated date on page one. An undated Oracle gets quoted with confidence two years after it stopped being true.

## Maintenance

Review per release and quarterly at minimum. Immediate update on: pricing or packaging change, a new capability, changed limits, a repositioning. A stale Oracle silently corrupts the whole library, because everything quotes it — the freshness audit exists to catch exactly this.

## The report (demo and engagement wrapper)

Header: `Product Oracle · N fields · N from evidence · <date>`.

**01 · The Oracle, drawn** — the two-pager rendered as a miniature artefact, sections visible, overview lines with their grades, TBD fields shown as dashed slots rather than hidden.

**02 · The field states** — counts: filled from evidence, filled from someone's word, TBD, with the TBDs traced to the decisions they wait on.

**03 · The finding** — one sentence, black band.

**04 · What can't be known** — always including: an Oracle is as current as its date line; TBD is a decision owed elsewhere, not a writing task; and the machine reader amplifies whatever is written, true or not.

## Drawing rules

- The Oracle rendered as the artefact itself, small — the reader should recognise a document, not read a description of one.
- Overview lines with grade marks in fill grammar. TBD slots dashed.
- Fragments in the drawing; the full sentences live in the file.
- Counts, never percentages under thirty.

## Language

| Use | Never |
|---|---|
| The document everyone else's document is built from | Single source of truth (once in the title is enough) |
| Who this is not for | Anti-ICP, negative persona |
| Filled from evidence, filled from someone's word, TBD | Verified, unverified, pending |
| The machine reader | LLM context, AI knowledge base |

## Standing rules for every output

- **Two pages, one owner, dated.**
- **Every overview claim carries its grade.** The Oracle consumes the Claim check, never re-argues it.
- **Point, never duplicate**: pricing, docs, battlecards are linked.
- **No invented findings. No false zeroes. No superlatives without proof.**
- **Read the source, never the snippet.**
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
- **Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes. No balanced two-part lines.** Written for a cold reader, and for the machine that reads over their shoulder.
