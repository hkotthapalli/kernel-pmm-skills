# Packaging and pricing

**Three decisions, in this order: the value metric, the packaging, then the price point.**

Most pricing work does only the third, or does all three backwards. The value metric is structural and nearly impossible to change once customers are on it. The price point is the easiest thing in the document to change later.

**This output does not invent a price.** Where real evidence exists it states the price point. Where it does not, it specifies the method that would produce one, and says plainly that the price is not known. A price nobody researched, written confidently in a document, is how companies end up underpriced for years.

## Two deliverables

**1. The report.** Sections 01 to 08 below — the decisions and the evidence.

**2. The price test kit.** Because the report's honest answer on the price point is usually "not known, here is the test", the test has to be runnable without you. The kit is a small working file: the willingness-to-pay interview prompts (five, fits inside any interview already happening), the four Van Westendorp questions formatted exactly as they should be asked, a tally sheet with the response threshold written on it, and one line on how to read the result. If the report prescribes a test and ships without the kit, the test never runs — only 22% of companies do any pricing research at all, and the missing piece is rarely willingness, it is a formatted thing to pick up. In the app, a button adds the five prompts to the Voice of customer interview guide.

On a real engagement both ship; for demo or portfolio use, the report alone is enough.

## What this is not

Not a competitor price comparison. That is separate work, and treating it as pricing strategy is the specific error the research warns about — when three companies calibrate to each other they converge on one number, and that number is usually too low for all of them. **Competitor pricing is information, not strategy.**

Not a cost calculation. Cost to serve tells you the floor below which you lose money. It tells you nothing about what someone will pay. Cost-plus is the default in most pricing guides and it is close to useless for software.

Not a price change plan. Communicating a change to existing customers is its own piece of work.

## The evidence this rests on

A 2026 survey of fifty B2B software leaders found underpricing was the most named regret, ahead of every other pricing mistake. In the same study, 62% had no single person accountable for pricing, only 22% did any rigorous research, and 90% let competitors shape their number. Pricing research tools (Conjointly, OpinionX, QuestionPro) run surveys and panels; none decides the value metric or packaging from the company's own positioning.

Two consequences for this method:

- **When the evidence gives a range, the top of the range is the default.** Going lower needs a written reason.
- **The output names one pricing owner.** Not a committee.

## Inputs

| Input | Comes from |
|---|---|
| Competitor pricing, and the value metric each uses | The Fifty, pricing pages read in full and dated |
| What buyers said about price, and their current spend | Voice of customer and Win / loss |
| Recorded loss reasons | Your CRM or notes, kept separate from the above |
| Target segment and buyer type | ICP and segments |
| Target customer and differentiator | Positioning |
| Fee and price claims already made | Claim check |
| Current pricing and cost to serve | You |

**Compare recorded loss reasons with buyer reasons.** Companies routinely record price as the loss reason far more often than buyers name it. If your records blame price three times as often as buyers do, pricing anxiety is not evidence and should not drive the price point.

## Decision one · Value metric

What you charge per: a seat, a month, a transaction, a percentage of assets, an outcome, a person, a device.

Test every candidate value metric against five tests:

| Test | The question |
|---|---|
| **Aligned** | Does it go up when the customer gets more value? |
| **Predictable** | Can the customer work out their bill before they get it? |
| **Defensible** | Can they shrink it without giving up value they want? |
| **Measurable** | Can you both see the number without arguing? |
| **Recognisable** | Is it a thing the buyer already counts? |

A value metric failing **aligned** eventually breaks the relationship. One failing **predictable** kills deals in procurement. The other three are recoverable.

**Check the value metric against the positioning.** This is the test nobody runs. If your target customer does not yet have the thing you charge on, the value metric is wrong — you would be charging a percentage of nothing, or per seat to a company with one user. When the value metric and the positioning disagree, one of them has to move, and the document says which.

**Say what the value metric rules out.** Every metric makes some customers unprofitable and some impossible to serve. Name them, and check them against the ICP beachhead segment.

## Decision two · Packaging

Three tiers is the ceiling at early stage. More than three and buyers stall choosing; fewer than two and you cannot expand.

For each tier:

| | |
|---|---|
| **For** | The segment, in the ICP's terms, never "small / medium / large" |
| **Includes** | The specific things |
| **Upgrade trigger** | The one capability that forces the move up |
| **Cost to serve** | Where the marginal cost is real, not near zero |

### Excluded features

Some capabilities are valued by very few buyers and lose deals when everyone is made to pay for them. The working threshold is anything wanted by under a fifth of buyers. These go in a tier nobody is forced into, or they do not ship. Listing excluded features prevents the tier sprawl that arrives eighteen months later.

### Unpriced commitments

**The section no other pricing method has, and the one that most often finds real money.**

List every free or unbounded commitment: "unlimited" anything, human time included at no charge, uncapped support, free onboarding, a trial with no end. For each, state the cost to serve per customer, what is charged for it, and how many buyers asked for it.

An uncapped promise involving human beings is a pricing decision made by accident. Either cap it, price it, or move it to the top tier — and say which.

**Draw cost to serve and charge on the same row, item by item.** The mismatch is usually the story: the thing that costs nothing is charged for, the thing that costs real money is free.

## Decision three · Price point

The output specifies the method. It states a price point only where evidence exists.

### Choosing the method

| Method | What it gives you | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Willingness-to-pay interview** | What they pay today for the competition, and what they would give up | Fold into interviews you are already running. Works at any size |
| **Van Westendorp survey** (four price questions) | A range, and where too cheap and too expensive cross | Cheapest survey option. Read it once 30 or more people answer; below that, report the raw answers |
| **Conjoint analysis** | Which combinations of features and prices people actually choose | Only at real scale. Expensive and slow |
| **Current spend** | The floor, and the anchor already in the buyer's head | Always available, always worth having |

**Start with the willingness-to-pay interview.** It costs nothing extra if interviews are already happening, and most companies skip it because it feels less rigorous than a survey they will never run. Vendor guides recommend 200 to 300 survey responses; at early stage, 30 is the minimum for a range and the result is labelled as directional.

Whichever method the report prescribes is the one the kit formats ready to run, not the whole menu.

### The Van Westendorp questions, when you use them

Ask about the same offer, in this order, open-ended:

1. At what price would this be so expensive you would not consider it?
2. At what price would it be so cheap you would doubt the quality?
3. At what price does it start to feel expensive, but still worth thinking about?
4. At what price would it be a bargain?

The answers plot as four lines. Where they cross gives a range and a midpoint, not an answer. **Report the range. Never report the midpoint alone.**

### Stating the price point

Every number carries its source and an evidence grade. A number with no source does not go in the document — write "Not known" and name the test.

## Evidence grades

Same fill grammar as every other output.

| Grade | Means | Drawn as |
|---|---|---|
| `Tested` | Came from research with buyers, and you could repeat it | Solid, green in the app |
| `Borrowed` | A competitor's price, a category benchmark, another market | Half |
| `Unknown` | Nobody has asked | Dashed outline |
| `Contradicted` | You state it and something of yours disagrees | Outlined, struck through |

Early-stage pricing is mostly unknown. Saying so is the honest result and it is what justifies the test plan.

## The key finding

The founder opened this document to answer one question: **is what I charge right, and if it isn't, what specifically is wrong with it.** The key finding answers that question and nothing else.

It is about the packaging or the price. Not about method, not about the market, not about what competitors do. Test every candidate: could the founder read this one line and know what to change this week? Findings that pass tend to be of the form:

- You charge for the thing that costs nothing and give away the thing that costs.
- You have never asked anyone what they would pay.
- Half your revenue depends on a tier nobody upgrades out of.

## Validation test and pricing owner

**The validation test.** A number and a date, written before the price ships. Usually one of: conversion at the new price, the share of deals discounted below a floor, or the proportion of buyers who raise price unprompted. Checkable by someone who was not in the room.

**Review trigger.** What makes this stale rather than wrong. Usually a competitor changing its value metric, the cost to serve moving, or the free thing you give away becoming the thing everyone wants.

**Pricing owner.** One role accountable for pricing. Where nobody owns it, write Unassigned and treat that as a result.

## Two buying motions

Where the same company sells two ways — B2C and B2B, or self-serve and purchased on behalf — the value metric and the packaging are usually different and must be worked separately. **One document, two blocks.** Never one merged model. State which decisions are shared and whether a customer can arrive through both.

## The report's structure

Header: `Packaging and pricing · <value metric> · N of N numbers tested · <sources> · <date>`

**01 · Competitor pricing** — price and value metric per competitor, drawn. Including free and the status quo.

**02 · Recorded loss reasons vs buyer reasons** — counted.

**03 · Value metric** — candidates against the five tests and the positioning check, one chosen, what it rules out.

**04 · Packaging** — tiers side by side, upgrade triggers, excluded features.

**05 · Unpriced commitments** — every unbounded promise, cost to serve against charge.

**06 · Price point** — what is known with its grade, what is not, and the test that would settle it.

**07 · Validation test** — with a date, the review trigger, and the pricing owner.

**08 · Limitations.**

Eight sections, plus the key finding in a black band. Two blocks in sections 03 to 06 where there are two buying motions.

## Drawing rules

- Counts as marks, filled out of the total.
- The five tests as a grid: candidates down, tests across, in fill, plus the positioning check column.
- Tiers drawn side by side.
- Cost to serve and charge drawn on the same row.
- Evidence grades in fill. Green only on Tested numbers, the chosen value metric, and approved decisions.
- A price range drawn as a bar with the range marked, never as a single point. An unknown range is a dashed bar with the anchors marked.

## Language

Standard product marketing terms on every label and heading. Never a label built from "who, what, which, where" phrasing. Where a method is named after a person, add a plain description beside it.

| Use | Instead of |
|---|---|
| Value metric | What you charge per, pricing axis |
| Packaging, tiers | What you sell |
| Upgrade trigger | What forces the upgrade |
| Excluded features | What you refuse to sell |
| Unpriced commitments | What you're giving away, cost leakage |
| Cost to serve | What it costs you |
| Price point, price range | The number |
| Recorded loss reasons vs buyer reasons | What you blamed, and what they said |
| Willingness-to-pay interview | The willingness-to-pay conversation |
| Van Westendorp survey (four price questions) | The four-question price survey |
| Conjoint analysis | Trade-off testing |
| Current spend | What they pay now |
| Price test kit | (stays) |
| Pricing owner | The owner |
| Evidence grades: Tested, Borrowed, Unknown, Contradicted | Unchecked |
| Not known | TBD, directionally, ballpark |
| Validation test, review trigger | Kill criterion, expiry condition |
| Key finding, Limitations | The finding, what can't be known |

## Voice

- Plain and direct. Their words in quotes, yours outside them.
- No slogans, no balanced two-part phrases, no line whose job is to sound clever.
- Never soften an unknown number into a confident one.
- Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes.

## Rules

- **Two deliverables: the report and the price test kit.**
- **The key finding answers the founder's question.** Is what I charge right, and what is wrong with it.
- **Value metric, then packaging, then price point.** Never the reverse.
- **Never invent a price.** State the method and mark it not known.
- **The top of the range is the default.** Going lower needs a written reason.
- **Competitor pricing is information, not strategy.**
- **Cost to serve sets the floor and nothing else.**
- **Check the value metric against the positioning.**
- **Every unpriced commitment gets costed against what you charge for it.**
- **Name one pricing owner.**
- **Three tiers maximum at early stage.**
- **Never a percentage under thirty.** Below 30 survey responses, report the raw answers.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.** Competitor pricing gets read on their own pricing page in full, including what is only visible after a click, and dated.
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
