# Landing page review

Reviews one page against four message layers, in order, and stops at the first one that fails. Produces a verdict per layer, a key finding, a fix paired to each failure, one headline rewrite, and the limitations. Then a 5-second test with five real people confirms or overturns the first two layers.

Turnaround is 24 hours for the read. It is free. Until the test answers arrive it is one experienced reader against a model, not a buyer panel, and the output must say so.

## The four message layers

| Order | Layer | The buyer's question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Clarity | What is it? |
| 2 | Relevance | Is it for me? |
| 3 | Value | Do I want it? |
| 4 | Differentiation | Why you over the competition? |

**The order is strict and failure is terminal.** A reader who cannot answer "is it for me" leaves. They never reach Value or Differentiation, so those layers were not evaluated — they were never seen. Mark them `NOT REACHED`, not `FAIL`.

This is the whole differentiator. Competitors score a list of 10 or 19 criteria with no theory of order. This tests a sequence and explains why the sequence matters. Do not turn it into a checklist.

## Verdicts

| Verdict | Means | Drawn as |
|---|---|---|
| `PASS` | A stranger gets it from the page alone | Solid block |
| `WEAK` | It's there but doing less work than it should | Block filled to half height |
| `FAIL` | Absent, or actively misleading | Outlined block, struck through |
| `NOT REACHED` | Sits after the first FAIL | Dashed outline, empty |

On screen the verdicts read Passes, Weak, Fails, Not reached.

**Verdicts are drawn in fill.** Solid means it's doing its job, half-filled means half, struck means failed, dashed means nobody got there. Green marks only a verdict that the 5-second test confirmed.

**A pass must be possible.** If every layer comes back WEAK or FAIL, re-read the page — a review that can never return a pass is a sales pitch. Say what works before saying what doesn't.

## Method

1. **Read the page as a stranger would**, above the fold first, in about five seconds. Write down what you think the product is before reading further. That first impression is the clarity evidence.
2. **Work the layers in order.** Stop assigning real verdicts after the first FAIL — everything below it is NOT REACHED.
3. **Check each layer against the client's own evidence where it exists.** Relevance against the target customer from Positioning or the ICP, value against the language bank from Voice of customer, differentiation against the Table-stakes read. Name the evidence source, or say the layer was judged by the reader alone.
4. **Quote the page.** Every verdict carries the actual words from the page, not a paraphrase. If the words aren't there, say so.
5. **Write the key finding.** One sentence naming the first layer that fails, in plain language. "Your page never says who it's for" — not "Relevance scores FAIL."
6. **Pair each failure with one priority fix**, one to one, in the order to make them. No fix without a failure above it.
7. **Write one headline rewrite.** The highest-leverage line on the page, usually the headline or the subhead. Show it before and after. One line only. When Positioning has homepage copy, take the line from there and say so.
8. **Write the limitations.** Always.
9. **Send the 5-second test** (below), and update the verdicts when five answers are in.

## The 5-second test

Five people outside the company who match the target customer and have not seen the site. Never the Voice of customer interviewees, who have seen it. The client sends a short message with a link. Each person sees the page above the fold for five seconds, then answers two questions:

1. What does this company sell?
2. Who is it for?

Kernel grades each answer right, partly or wrong against the product and the target customer, and the client can overrule a grade. Once five answers are in, clarity and relevance take their verdict from the answers: a pass needs four of five right. Value and differentiation stay with the reader's judgement, because five seconds is too short for them. Show the five answers to question one, grouped and counted.

Five people is enough to show a large problem. It is not enough to compare two good headlines. Say so.

## Output structure

Six sections, in this order, plus the 5-second test once answers arrive. Do not add others.

1. **The page** — drawn as a wireframe, each zone in its verdict fill state
2. **The four layers** — each with verdict, quoted evidence and evidence source, in order, with the terminal break drawn as a rule across the page
3. **Key finding** — one sentence, in a black band
4. **Priority fixes** — each paired to its failure, numbered in the order to make them
5. **Headline rewrite** — before and after
6. **Limitations**
7. **5-second test** — once answers arrive: the answers, the grades, the grouped answers to question one, and the verdicts they confirmed

Before the test, sections 1 to 6 are labelled as one reader's judgement. After it, the test results sit beside each verdict, and anything they confirm turns green.

Every run is kept, so the client can see whether the first failure moved further down after a change to the page.

The header carries the URL and the date.

The limitations always include: until the 5-second test is in, this is one reader and a model and no real buyer has seen it; it reviews the page, not the product; and it cannot tell you whether the traffic arriving is the right traffic.

## Language

Standard product marketing terms on every label and heading. Never a label built from "who, what, which, where" phrasing.

| Use | Instead of |
|---|---|
| Clarity, Relevance, Value, Differentiation | (the four message layers) |
| Passes, Weak, Fails, Not reached | (verdicts) |
| Key finding | The finding, the one thing |
| Priority fix | What to fix first, fixes |
| Headline rewrite | One line rewritten, one line before and after |
| Evidence source | Checked against |
| 5-second test | (stays) |
| Competition | Alternatives |
| Limitations | What this can't tell you |

## Voice

- Plain and direct. Say what is on the page and what isn't.
- No slogans, no balanced two-part phrases, no sentences that sound like a conclusion but state nothing.
- Never write a line whose job is to sound clever.
- Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes.
- Address the reader as "you" and the page as "your page".
- Never soften a FAIL into advice. Say it failed, then say what to do.

## Rules

- **Every claim about the page carries the page's own words.** No unquoted assertions.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.** Fetch and read the actual page before reviewing it. A search-result summary, a cached description or a screenshot of the fold is a lead, not evidence. This applies doubly to any claim about a competitor's page — read theirs in full too. If a claim cannot be traced to something read end to end, it does not go in the output.
- **Never invent a metric.** Do not say "you're losing X% of readers" — nobody measured it. The layers are a judgment, not a funnel.
- **Fill first.** Verdicts are fill states. Green marks only a verdict the 5-second test confirmed. The key finding sits in a black band.
- **Do not pitch inside the review.** The review ends at section 7. Any next step lives outside the deliverable.
- **If the page is good, say so and stop.** A short honest review is worth more than a long manufactured one.
