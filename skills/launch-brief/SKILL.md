# Launch brief

A checklist says what to make. The brief says what this launch is trying to achieve, for whom, and how everyone will know whether it worked. Teams that only have the checklist ship a lot of assets and can never answer whether the launch succeeded.

The tier decides the size (Launch tiers). The brief decides the substance. One brief per launch, one page.

## Two deliverables

**1. The brief-as-tracker.** One page on the client's own drive: the goal, audiences, messages, channels, gates, owners, and a status column. It is the working document until ship — updated weekly, exceptions only. Near launch week the only statuses allowed are `Ready`, `Blocked` and `Not started`. "In progress" seven days out is a risk wearing a costume.

**2. The report.** The brief for a real launch, judged, with the gaps shown as gaps.

On a real engagement both ship; for demo or portfolio use, the report alone is enough.

## What this brief refuses to contain

The most widely used launch checklist in the discipline carries ninety tasks across twelve categories, and inside it sit market analysis, persona interviews, pricing analysis and positioning. That model rebuilds the whole function every launch.

This brief contains none of that. Research, personas, pricing and positioning are standing outputs that already exist upstream. The brief consumes them. Where an input is missing — no pricing decision, no tested messaging, no tier assigned — it is written into the brief as a named blocker, never done inline. **Writing new positioning inside a launch brief is how a company ends up with launch-specific claims nobody maintains.**

## The goal: one number, agreed before ship

Ask: three months after launch, what number tells us this worked? Push until it is specific, dated, and checkable by someone who was not in the room — the assumption log's pass-number form.

If nobody will commit, that is a finding, not a gap to fill: write `No success number agreed — [who declined]`. A launch with no agreed number has no agreed purpose, and its retro will be an argument.

## Audiences

Priority ordered, and honestly unequal. For each: what they believe now, what they need to come to believe, what they should do. That three-part frame is what stops "awareness" being an objective.

Include the internal audience explicitly — whoever will be asked about the launch by a customer. For a company that sells through someone else, internal includes the partner's people: a B2B2C product's sales force is the employer's HR team, and they belong inside the gate.

## Messages

Rows copied from the messaging matrix, proof states carried along. One message per audience. If the launch needs a line the matrix does not contain, the matrix gets updated first and the brief waits.

Test before ship: the market's own playbooks say messaging goes in front of three to five customers before it is finalised. Where the client has a community, that is who tests it.

## Channels

Each channel traces to a named audience; a tactic serving no audience is activity and is the first thing cut when the timeline slips. Plan distribution up front, not as an afterthought. Give the community, partners and early customers **early access with material of their own to share** — a preview and something unique, never a canned post.

## The gates

**Internal before external, always.** One to two weeks before the public date: the people who will be asked can answer. Completion is evidence, not status — material delivered, the session run, the top three objections answered cold. Without evidence, status turns political.

**Launch-day order: internal, then affected customers, then public.** In that sequence. The most common launch failure in the field is not a weak campaign; it is a prospect who saw the announcement and reached a person who had not heard of it.

**The 48-hour go/no-go.** A named person with authority to delay, deciding two days out. A launch delayed two weeks is fine. A launch delayed the day before, after the emails are queued, is a crisis.

**The point of no return.** The date after which slipping costs more than shipping imperfectly, marked on the timeline.

## Risks: the pre-mortem plugs in

Two weeks before ship, the Pre-mortem output runs (or re-runs) on this launch, and its tripwires attach to the brief. The brief does not carry its own freestyle risks section; it carries the tripwire list and who watches each one.

## Reviews, pre-scheduled

Day 7 (early signal: activation, tickets, what confused people), day 30 (the formal check against the pass number), day 90 (adoption, proof collected from early users). Scheduled in the brief before launch so they cannot be quietly skipped. They feed the Launch post-mortem output.

## What we're deliberately not doing

Short and unusually useful. Scope discipline written down stops the launch accreting tactics in week three, and gives the owner something to point at. At a two-founder company this is the most important section in the brief.

## One owner

A single directly responsible person for the launch overall. A launch with two owners has none. Every line has an owner; `[Owner TBD]` is acceptable for a week, blank never.

## The report's structure

Header: `Launch brief · <the launch> · tier N · <date>`.

**01 · The goal** — the pass number, its date, who agreed it, drawn large. Or the refusal, recorded.

**02 · Who has to move** — audiences as rows: believe now, need to believe, should do, with the matrix row each message came from and its proof state mark.

**03 · The gates and the calendar** — the back-planned timeline drawn as a bar: internal gate, go/no-go, launch day, day 7/30/90 marks, point of no return flagged.

**04 · Blockers and what we're not doing** — upstream gaps as named blockers; the not-doing list.

**05 · The finding** — one sentence, black band.

**06 · What can't be known** — standing limits, always including: a pass number set before launch is a bet; the brief is as current as its last weekly pass.

## Drawing rules

- The timeline is the main visual: one horizontal bar, gates as vertical marks, the point of no return as the heaviest mark.
- Audience rows carry the proof state marks from the matrix, in fill grammar.
- Blockers drawn as dashed slots, named.
- No colour. Counts, never percentages under thirty.

## Language

| Use | Never |
|---|---|
| The pass number | Success metrics, KPIs, north star |
| Who can delay it | Escalation authority |
| The people who will be asked | Internal stakeholders |
| What we're deliberately not doing | Descoped, out of scope |
| Ready, blocked, not started | In progress, on track |

## Standing rules for every output

- **Two deliverables: the brief-as-tracker and the report.**
- **The brief consumes upstream outputs, never rebuilds them.** Missing inputs are blockers with names.
- **No invented numbers.** A refused pass number is recorded, not replaced.
- **Internal before external, always.** If the timeline does not allow it, the launch date is wrong.
- **Read the source, never the snippet.**
- **Name organisations, never individuals**, when the work is confidential.
- **Sentence case. British spelling. No em dashes. No balanced two-part lines.** Written for a cold reader.
